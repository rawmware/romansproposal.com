Small business automation guide · By Roman

What should a small business automate first?

Start with a repeated task that has a clear trigger, a predictable next step, and someone responsible when it fails. Lead follow-up, appointment booking, and intake are useful places to look.

1. Find the delay before choosing a tool

Write down the path from a new inquiry to a booked conversation. Who reads the inquiry? Where is it recorded? What information is missing? How long does the customer wait? Choose the slowest repeatable handoff you can actually measure.

For example, if an agent copies every website inquiry into a CRM, begin with transferring the inquiry and assigning an owner. Adding a chatbot would not fix that missing handoff by itself.

2. Match the workflow to the problem

New leads wait too long for a reply

Send an acknowledgment after an inquiry, create a CRM record, and alert the person who will respond. Include a fallback for failed delivery and duplicate submissions. Measure time to a human response, not just time to an automated message.

Booking takes too many emails

Connect an appointment form to an availability calendar. Define time zones, buffers, rescheduling rules, and what happens when the calendar is unavailable. Measure completed bookings and the staff time spent arranging them.

Intake information arrives incomplete

Use a structured form with required fields and an internal review step. Collect only information needed at this stage. For a law firm, keep suitability and conflict decisions with the responsible staff; a submitted form is not an accepted engagement.

The same questions interrupt staff all day

An AI assistant may help answer questions from approved business information. Give it an explicit handoff when an answer is missing, and test outdated prices, ambiguous requests, and unsupported questions before launch. It should not invent availability or make commitments on your behalf.

3. Decide whether AI is needed

A fixed confirmation email, calendar rule, or transfer between systems usually needs ordinary automation. AI becomes useful when the input varies, such as summarizing a free-text inquiry for a staff member. Keep consequential decisions and uncertain outputs reviewable.

4. Calculate the value with your own numbers

Estimate monthly hours saved as: tasks per week × minutes saved per task × 4.3 ÷ 60. Subtract time spent reviewing results and handling failures.

For an illustrative workflow with 20 tasks each week and five minutes saved per task, that is about seven hours per month before review time. Compare the value of those hours with setup, hosting, messaging, AI usage, and maintenance costs. This is a planning example, not a promised result.

5. Prepare a brief a builder can use

  • Trigger: What starts the workflow?
  • Inputs: Which forms, calendars, inboxes, or CRM records does it use?
  • Output: What should happen, and who owns the next action?
  • Exceptions: What happens with missing information, duplicates, or downtime?
  • Access: Who owns the accounts and can remove access?
  • Success: Which baseline and follow-up measurement will show improvement?

Build one useful workflow first

Test a small version with representative examples, including failures. Review the results with the people doing the work before expanding it. A workflow that reliably removes one bottleneck gives you a clearer starting point for the next improvement.
